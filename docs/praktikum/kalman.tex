\begin{abstract}
\noindent
You estimate, you do not drive. A grader places your robot at the start, drives it
through a fixed command sequence and records both the exact pose
(\thema{truth}) and your estimate \thema{kf/pose}. Grading is on accuracy:
RMSE against the truth, improvement ratio over the raw sensor, maximum error
during a GPS outage and consistency of the standard deviation you publish
(NEES). For that you build a Kalman filter with a state vector of position and
velocity, feed it GPS (slow, noisy, sometimes missing), odometry
(fast, drifting) and IMU (clean short-term, worthless long-term), and report
\emph{how} uncertain you are. The simulator is the one from experiment 1 — new are
an IMU noise model, three topics and the question of whether your numbers are right.
\end{abstract}

\section{Experiment goal and prelab questions}\label{sec:vorfragen}
\subsection{Goal}
After this experiment you can
\begin{itemize}
  \item predict a state vector \((x,y,v_x,v_y)\) with a CV model and fold in every
        measurement with its own uncertainty — prediction, update and
        Kalman gain from \(P\), \(Q\) and \(R\), not from guesswork,
  \item state your own uncertainty so that it is \emph{right}, and check it with the
        normalized error squared (NEES),
  \item use the odometry \emph{twist} and the gyro rate as a motion model to bridge a
        ten-second GPS outage below 1.8\,m error,
  \item separate drift (correlated, growing) from measurement noise (white, zero-mean)
        and justify why an IMU is never integrated open-loop,
  \item build RMSE time series, a \(Q\)/\(R\) table and a
        NEES discussion for the lab report from a measurement log.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theory: predict, measure, weigh}\label{sec:theorie}
\subsection{State, prediction, update, covariance}
The filter knows a state vector \(\boldsymbol\xi\) and its uncertainty, the covariance
\(P\). Here \(\boldsymbol\xi=(x,y,v_x,v_y)^{\mathsf T}\) in world meters is enough at
first; K4 asks for one more degree of freedom for the turn rate. The cycle always runs
the same way: \textbf{prediction} over the real elapsed time \(dt\) since the last state,
\textbf{update} as soon as a measurement \(z\) with its uncertainty \(R\) arrives.
\begin{align}
  \boldsymbol\xi^{-} &= F\,\boldsymbol\xi + \text{inputs},
   && P^{-} = F\,P\,F^{\mathsf T} + Q
   \label{eq:pradiktion}\\
  \mathbf y &= z - H\,\boldsymbol\xi^{-},
   && S = H\,P^{-}H^{\mathsf T} + R,\quad K = P^{-}H^{\mathsf T}S^{-1}
   \label{eq:innovation}\\
  \boldsymbol\xi^{+} &= \boldsymbol\xi^{-} + K\,\mathbf y,
   && P^{+} = (I-KH)P^{-}(I-KH)^{\mathsf T} + KRK^{\mathsf T}
   \label{eq:update}
\end{align}
For a directly measured quantity \(K = P^{-}/(P^{-}+R)\): if the prediction is more
uncertain than the measurement, the filter pulls toward the measurement; if \(P\) is
small, little happens. The two knobs mean different things: \(Q\) says what the
\emph{model} lacks (unexplained acceleration), \(R\) what the \emph{sensor} lacks. Raising
only \(Q\) makes the filter twitchy; raising only \(R\) makes it sluggish. Both set the
covariance — and with it the numbers the grade is computed from (impr. = improvement ratio):
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{NEES} = \frac1N\sum_i \frac12\!
     \left(\frac{e_{x,i}^{2}}{s_{x,i}^{2}} + \frac{e_{y,i}^{2}}{s_{y,i}^{2}}\right),\quad
  \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\langle e_x^2+e_y^2\rangle},\quad
  \text{impr.} = \frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_{\text{GPS}}}{\mathrm{RMSE}_{\text{KF}}}.
  \label{eq:nees}
\end{equation}
With two degrees of freedom the expected NEES value is 1; a single value up to about 5.99
is still unremarkable under a \(\chi^2\) distribution. Without \cref{eq:update} in Joseph
form \(P\) tends to stay asymmetric after rounding errors — for a 4×4 filter by hand
probably the most common silent source of NEES far above 1.

\subsection{The CV model in matrices}
Constant velocity is what the model is called, because it does not know acceleration. In
matrices (world frame, wheel order irrelevant, state \((x,y,v_x,v_y)\)):
\begin{equation}
  F(dt) = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&dt&0\\0&1&0&dt\\0&0&1&0\\0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
  H = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\0&1&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
  R = \begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{\text{gps}}^2&0\\0&\sigma_{\text{gps}}^2\end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:cv}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  Q(dt) \;=\; q\,
  \begin{pmatrix}\frac{dt^4}{4}&0&\frac{dt^3}{2}&0\\[2pt]
                0&\frac{dt^4}{4}&0&\frac{dt^3}{2}\\[2pt]
                  \frac{dt^3}{2}&0&dt^2&0\\[2pt]
                0&\frac{dt^3}{2}&0&dt^2\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad q\ \mathrm{in\ m^2/s^3}.
  \label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
\(q\) is the assumed uncertainty of the \emph{acceleration}, that is, of what the
CV model cannot represent; a usable start is \(0.3\ldots1.0\), recommended in
\texttt{kf.q\_acc} = 0.6 (\cref{tab:parameter}). \(\sigma_{\text{gps}}\) is not
guessed but read from the sensor profile
(\kommando{rob.config("gps.sigma\_xy", 0.5)} or \thema{/sim/config}). Without numpy, hand
matrix multiplication is enough: \cref{eq:cv} is sparse, \cref{eq:q}
block-wise — four lines of code per axis. Two things decide good or bad:
\(dt\) is the \emph{real} difference of the message stamps (not an averaged fixed value),
and the filter predicts up to the \emph{measurement time} of the incoming measurement
before it updates. At 5\,Hz GPS and 0.85\,m/s, 100\,ms of lag costs 8.5\,cm — already
a third of the K4 target.

\subsection{Why the gyro helps short-term and odometry long-term}
Two facts determine the architecture of the filter. \textbf{First:} an IMU measures
specific force, and its error grows with the integration order. The values from
\cref{tab:parameter} say: gyro noise \(10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad/s/\sqrt{Hz}}\) is
\(7.1\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) per sample — negligible; the gyro bias
\(5\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) gives \(0.05\,\mathrm{rad}\) of heading error in
10\,s (\(2.9^{\circ}\)), which costs \(0.25\,\mathrm{m}\) of cross-track error over
10\,m of driving. For the accelerometer a \(0.05\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) bias becomes
\(\tfrac12 b t^2 = 2.5\,\mathrm{m}\) of position error in 10\,s through
\emph{two} integrations. Hence: turn rate yes (one integration, small), acceleration only
short-term and never open-loop — the IMU is a motion model for fractions of a second,
not a positioning device. \textbf{Second:} odometry delivers two different things. Its
\emph{pose} is dead reckoning and drifts (random walk from \(\sigma_\text{wheel}\),
rotated by the heading error). Its \emph{twist} \((v_x,v_y,\omega)\), however, is a
direct measurement: with \(r=0.05\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(\sigma_\text{wheel}=0.04\) to
\(0.05\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) the value \(v_x=\tfrac{r}{4}\sum_i w_i\) carries only
\(\approx 1\,\mathrm{mm/s}\) of noise \emph{without} drift. The workable filter therefore
predicts with the \emph{measured} body velocity (odometry twist, rotated into the world
frame) and the gyro rate, and updates the position with GPS. Anyone who instead throws the
odometry \emph{pose} in as a measurement with a fixed \(R\) doubles information that is
already in the prediction — the third way is to carry the drift as its own state
value (bias) and let \(R\) grow with time.

\subsection{GPS: noise, bias, GPS outage, model error}
GPS is the only absolute sensor. Its noise is correlated in an unknown way, so it is
practically made for \(R\): \(\sigma_{xy}=0.5\,\mathrm{m}\) in K1/K3,
\(0.8\,\mathrm{m}\) in K2, \(0.6\,\mathrm{m}\) in K4 — the raw RMSE sits at
\(\sigma\sqrt2\approx0.7\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(1.1\,\mathrm{m}\), and the
improvement ratio in \cref{eq:nees} is computed against exactly that number. A
\emph{fixed} bias (\texttt{gps.bias\_xy}, multi-path) is unobservable from GPS alone:
no filter can remove it, but you can carry it as a constant uncertainty inside
\(R\); \texttt{gps.bias\_step} adds it on top for one time window — the outlier drill.
The \textbf{GPS outage} finally (\texttt{gps.gap = [15, 8]} in K2) drains the
measurement side completely: prediction keeps running, the covariance grows only
through \(Q\), and the error grows with the elapsed time — bounded by the model error,
since a CV model cannot predict a turn at \(0.26\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). So K2 does not grade
the mean RMSE first, but the maximum error inside the gap (\(\le 1.8\,\mathrm{m}\)):
anyone who takes the gyro turn rate into the state vector survives the ten seconds;
anyone who runs pure CV drives straight into the arena corner.

\textbf{Why \thema{az} reads \(\approx +9.81\) at rest.} A MEMS accelerometer measures no
motion but specific force — the force per mass that keeps it from falling freely. On flat
ground that is the normal force pointing up, i.e. \(+g\). That is neither a measurement
error nor a sign bug, but the physics of the chip: anyone who integrates \thema{az} as it is
ends up 490\,m high after 10\,s. Only \thema{ax} and \thema{ay} matter in the plane — and
those are biased, tilted and superimposed, which is why they are only usable short-term.
\thema{gx}/\thema{gy} pick up the same tilt, \thema{gz} is the main signal in 2D.

\textbf{Measure instead of believing.} A standstill run over 5\,s (seed 1, robot
\emph{alice}, 502 samples) gives \thema{az} \(=9.74\pm0.014\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) — the spread is
exactly the computed density, the difference to 9.81 is the \thema{accel\_bias} of the
\(z\)-axis — and a gyro mean of \(-5.4\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). That mean is your
calibration: average the IMU at standstill (before the drive or in every pause), subtract
the mean — that is how the calibration is done later on the real robot.

\subsection{Three rules that save the run}
\textbf{First: message stamp, not wall clock.} Every measurement carries \thema{t} — the
simulation time in which it was created. Use those stamps for \(dt\), for \(Q\) and for
whether a measurement has already been processed. Anyone who instead calls
\kommando{time.time()} gets a wrong \(dt\) in the grading: the speed-up script
\ordner{tools/fastgrade.py} packs 25 simulation seconds into one wall-clock second,
and your filter then reads a 5-second gap as 200 milliseconds.
\emph{Second: you estimate, you do not drive.} In the KF tasks the grader sets
\thema{cmd\_vel}, the simulation drives the command sequence from \ordner{config/tasks.json}
blind. Your node reads sensors and answers on \thema{kf/pose} —
\kommando{send\_wheels()}, \kommando{publish\_cmd\_vel()} and \kommando{rob.truth()} are
forbidden in this experiment (the grader notices this in the missing drive and the contact
count), otherwise it judges a drive you never made.

\emph{Third: a new task really starts from scratch.} When \kommando{rob.task()}
changes, the robot is dropped at the spawn pose and the sensor profile switches. End your
measurement loop then, read \kommando{rob.sensor\_profil()} again and — the case that costs
the most points — discard the \emph{last} measurement of the previous task: the bus keeps
the last message, and a GPS fix from before the respawn is a lie for the new task. Check
\thema{fix.t} against the start of your mission (the reference solution keeps
\thema{KF.start} for that).
Two things follow from the same fact, and both are in the reference solution. The \emph{first}
measurement of the new task must also be newer than everything the bus already knew when your
\kommando{mission()} began — note that baseline of stamps at the start and wait for a stamp
above it, otherwise your filter initialises on the pose of the last drive, several metres
away. And a step of more than about two seconds is not a prediction, it is a node that did not
run: do not extrapolate five seconds of CV model over it, start the filter again on that
measurement.
You may repeat the gyro bias calibration — but the state and
\(P\) do not belong at zero, otherwise you throw away information you only just
gathered.

\section{Tasks K1 to K4}\label{sec:aufgaben}
All four tasks run the same logic: the grader puts your robot at the spawn pose, drives the
command sequence stored in \ordner{config/tasks.json}, records
\thema{truth} and \thema{kf/pose} and compares. Measurement only starts after the settling
time (4\,s), so that your standstill bias calibration counts. \cref{tab:aufgaben} lists the
grading limits; \kommando{./lab grade --task kf\_alle --controller student/kf\_template.py}
shows you beforehand the same numbers that count in the submission.

\begin{description}
  \item[K1 — Position estimate from GPS alone.] Build the CV model
    \(\boldsymbol\xi=(x,y,v_x,v_y)^{\mathsf T}\) with \(F(dt)\) and the acceleration noise
    \(Q\) integrated over \(dt\) (\cref{eq:cv,eq:q}), then update with
    the GPS position \emph{at the stamp} \thema{fix.t}. The goal is understanding the
    recursion, not the bag of tricks: a single, honest KF is enough.
  \item[K2 — Fuse GPS, odometry and IMU.] With 1 Hz GPS and \(0.8\) m spread the
    \emph{absolute} error is noise-limited — so grading looks at the improvement over the
    raw sensor signal. The odometry \thema{twist} helps as a prediction input (rotated into
    the world) and the gyro for the turn rate — both carrying a bias that first has to be
    measured at standstill. During the outage only the motion model is left: this is where
    it shows whether you ground \(Q\) in physics or guess it.
  \item[K3 — Knowing how well you estimate.] The same drive as K1, but graded is the
    \emph{reported} uncertainty: \thema{kf/pose} carries \(\sigma_x,\sigma_y\) from \(P\).
    Increase \(Q\) when the NEES runs above 3; decrease it when it drops below 0.3
    — and write the trade-off into the lab report.
  \item[K4 — Fast drive, faithful estimate.] 0.85\,m/s with a sine oscillation added to
    the turn rate, IMU at 200\,Hz. This is where it pays off that your filter runs at the
    \emph{measurement rate} instead of a fixed loop rate.
\end{description}

Typical error patterns to recognise your implementation by: the estimate trails the
measurement by a constant amount (\(F\) without \(dt\), \(Q=0\)); the covariance shrinks and
never grows again (Joseph form or the \(P\) update forgotten); the robot is rotated by the
gyro although the world needs the heading (sign/rotation of the body
velocity); the GPS outage is bridged with an \(R\) of \(10^{-6}\) (then
the estimate follows the odometry blindly and \cref{tab:aufgaben} K2 fails).

\section{Evaluation and lab report}\label{sec:bericht}
The lab report is yours — but the evaluation produces numbers you have to explain. It
expects:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Trajectory and error signature.} One plot from \ordner{tools/kfplot.py}: truth,
    raw GPS, odometry, your estimate — and below it the error against truth over time.
    Describe \emph{where} the error grows (curves? GPS outage? after the task switch?).
  \item \textbf{A \(Q\)/\(R\) table.} Which values did you use, how did you arrive at them
    (density \(\to\) \(Q\), \thema{gps.sigma\_xy} \(\to\) \(R\)), and what happens when you
    scale them by a factor of 3 in both directions? Four measured points suffice — but they
    have to be measurements, not guesses.
  \item \textbf{NEES discussion for K3.} Your mean value, its spread within the run, and what
    you changed to get inside the band.
  \item \textbf{Measured IMU bias drift.} Average \thema{imu} at standstill over 10\,s before
    and after the run, report the difference per axis, and compare it with
    \cref{tab:parameter}. From that follows your argument for why the IMU is not
    integrated open loop.
  \item \textbf{Value of odometry during the GPS outage.} RMSE inside the outage with and
    without odometry prediction (two measurement logs, one concluding sentence). If you claim
    the model is enough on its own — measure it.
  \item \textbf{One sentence on the model choice.} K4 drives faster and oscillates: which
    state variable would you still have needed (turn rate in the state? bias as a state?),
    and what would it have cost?
\end{itemize}
Work from the measurement log, not from the GUI window: \kommando{--log messung.csv}
writes truth, gps, odom, kf, imu and the velocities into a CSV file that you can evaluate
without matplotlib too (\kommando{-\-list} lists the columns).

\section{Formula sheet}\label{sec:formeln}
\textbf{CV model (straight line, world frame):}
\[
  F(dt)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&dt&0\\0&1&0&dt\\0&0&1&0\\0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\qquad
  Q(dt)=q\!\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{dt^3}{3}&0&\tfrac{dt^2}{2}&0\\[2pt]
        0&\tfrac{dt^3}{3}&0&\tfrac{dt^2}{2}\\[2pt]
        \tfrac{dt^2}{2}&0&dt&0\\[2pt]0&\tfrac{dt^2}{2}&0&dt\end{pmatrix}
\]
with the acceleration density \(q\) in m²/s³ — the simulator starts with
\(q=0.6\) (\thema{kf.q\_acc}), the turn rate with \(q_{\text{gier}}=0.02\).

\textbf{Recursion:}
\(\boldsymbol\xi^{-}=F\boldsymbol\xi\), \(P^{-}=FPF^{\mathsf T}+Q\),
\(y=z-H\boldsymbol\xi^{-}\), \(S=HP^{-}H^{\mathsf T}+R\),
\(K=P^{-}H^{\mathsf T}S^{-1}\), \(\boldsymbol\xi=\boldsymbol\xi^{-}+Ky\),
\(P=(I-KH)P^{-}\). For the position measurement \(H\) is the selection matrix with the two
ones in the \(x\) and \(y\) columns, \(R=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{\text{gps}}^2,
\sigma_{\text{gps}}^2)\) with \(\sigma_{\text{gps}}\) from \thema{gps.sigma\_xy}.

\textbf{Conversions you may look up:}
White density \(d\) [unit/\(\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\)] \(\to\) spread per sample
\(d\sqrt{\text{rate}/2}\). Bias random walk \(w\) \(\to\) spread per step
\(w/\sqrt{\text{rate}}\), after \(T\) seconds \(w\sqrt{T}\). A constant bias \(b\) over \(T\)
seconds, integrated twice: position error \(\tfrac{1}{2}bT^2\).

\textbf{Consistency:}
\(\overline{\text{NEES}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i\big[(e_{x,i}^2/\sigma_{x,i}^2
  + e_{y,i}^2/\sigma_{y,i}^2)\big/2\big]\);
expected value 1, a single value up to 5.99 is still unremarkable with two degrees of freedom
(\(\chi^2\), 95\,\%). Report \(\sigma_x=\sqrt{P_{11}}\) and
\(\sigma_y=\sqrt{P_{22}}\) — not the square root of the trace.

\section{Solutions to the prelab questions}\label{sec:loesungen}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{IMU.} One sample: \(\sigma_a=0.002\cdot\sqrt{100/2}
        = 0.014\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) (14\,mm/s²). The factor \(\sqrt{\text{rate}/2}\) comes
        from integrating the density over the equivalent noise bandwidth — with a
        sampling rate \(f_s\) that is \(f_s/2\). Constant bias \(b=0.05\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\):
        after \(T=10\)\,s the speed error is \(\Delta v=bT=0.5\)\,m/s and the position error
        \(\tfrac{1}{2}bT^2=2.5\)\,m. Over the same time white noise contributes only
        \(\sigma_a T^2 N^{3/2}/\sqrt{3}\approx 0.03\)\,m (\(N=1000\) samples) — the bias
        dominates by two orders of magnitude. \emph{Consequence:} the IMU serves as a
        \emph{motion model} with short memory, not as a position sensor; the bias has to be
        estimated (or at least made small by averaging at standstill).
  \item \textbf{Recursion.} With \(dt=0.5\) and \(q=0.6\):
        \[
          FP F^{\mathsf T}=\begin{pmatrix}0.04+dt^2\cdot1.0 & dt\cdot1.0\\ dt\cdot1.0 & 1.0\end{pmatrix}
          =\begin{pmatrix}0.29&0.50\\0.50&1.00\end{pmatrix},\quad
          Q=0.6\begin{pmatrix}0.0417&0.125\\0.125&0.5\end{pmatrix},
        \]
        so \(P^{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0.315&0.575\\0.575&1.30\end{pmatrix}\) and
        \(S=0.315+0.25=0.565\). Gain
        \(K=P^{-}H^{\mathsf T}/S=(0.315/0.565,\;0.575/0.565)=(0.56,\;1.02)\).
        Innovation \(y=2.40-2.00=0.40\), hence
        \(\hat x^{+}=2.00+0.56\cdot0.40=2.22\)\,m and
        \(\hat v^{+}=0+1.02\cdot0.40=0.41\)\,m/s — the second gain entry may be larger
        than 1, because it acts on a velocity. Position uncertainty:
        \(\sigma^{-}=\sqrt{0.315}=0.56\,\mathrm{m}\) before,
        \(\sigma^{+}=\sqrt{0.315-0.56^2\cdot0.565}=0.37\,\mathrm{m}\) after the update.
  \item \textbf{Odometry drift.} The odometry pose error is the sum of all errors made
        so far: it is correlated in time, it grows with the distance driven, and it keeps
        its sign (a wheel radius that is too large stays too large). White noise with a
        fixed \(R\) describes an error that is forgotten with every measurement instead.
        Result: the filter takes each new odometry pose for an independent measurement,
        \(P\) shrinks toward zero, the covariance becomes too small — the NEES climbs far
        above 1, and during the GPS outage the estimate drifts along unchecked. The right
        way: use the odometry as an \emph{input} (velocity) in the prediction, and model
        the remainder as process noise \(Q\) (growing with \(dt\)) or as an additional
        bias state.
  \item \textbf{NEES.} \(0.1\): the reported standard deviation is on average
        \(\sqrt{1/0.1}\approx3.2\) times too large — the filter is needlessly cautious and
        trusts the measurement too little. \(1\): consistent, the spread of the errors matches
        the claim. \(8\): too small by \(\sqrt{8}\approx2.8\) times — overconfident, and
        therefore dangerous, because the application (control, safety) relies on the claim.
        The band \([0.05;\,3.5]\) for K3 rejects \(0.01\) and \(8\) — being cautious is
        tolerated (a paced run of the reference solution measures 0.09 to 0.9), being
        over-confident is not — only \(1\)
        survives it. (The lower edge is deliberately loose — \(0.15\) corresponds to a claim
        \(2.6\) times too large: casual, but not dangerous. Above \(3.5\) it gets tight,
        because confident wrong claims help nobody in the lab.)
\end{enumerate}
